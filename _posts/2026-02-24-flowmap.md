Diffusion models generate images by repeatedly predicting how a noisy sample should change. Each prediction is local; the sampler combines many of them into a trajectory. **Flow maps learn the result of an entire time interval**, making one-step or few-step generation possible.

The central problem is learning a useful large jump. We will build from noise prediction and velocity fields to finite-time maps, then look at how MeanFlow learns these maps without computing training trajectories. Finally, we will separate improvements to the jump itself from methods that improve the distribution of generated images and videos.

Throughout, $x_0$ is data and $x_1$ is noise. Generation runs from $t=1$ toward $t=0$. We define velocity as $v=dx/dt$, so a generation step uses a negative time increment.

<h2 id="noise-and-velocity">1. From noise to a velocity field</h2>

### Diffusion: learn the direction back to data

[Diffusion](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11239) training starts with a clean example and adds Gaussian noise. A schedule sets the signal amplitude $\alpha_t$ and noise amplitude $\sigma_t$:

$$
x_t=\alpha_t x_0+\sigma_t\epsilon,
\qquad \epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I).
$$

A denoiser learns to predict the added noise from $(x_t,t)$. Because multiple noise–data pairs can produce the same noisy input, squared-error training learns the conditional mean noise, $\mathbb E[\epsilon\mid x_t]$. This prediction determines the **score**, $s_t(x)=\nabla_x\log p_t(x)$: a vector pointing toward increasing density at that noise level. Together with the noise schedule, the score defines a [probability-flow ordinary differential equation (ODE)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13456), giving a deterministic route from noise to data. Sampling then becomes a numerical integration problem.

### Flow Matching: learn velocity directly

[Flow Matching](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.02747) trains the ODE velocity itself. One common construction draws independent data and noise endpoints and connects them with a straight segment:

$$
x_t=(1-t)x_0+tx_1,\qquad v_c=x_1-x_0.
$$

The sampled segment has constant velocity $v_c$, so training can supervise any intermediate point without running a solver. The figure shows the resulting distributions as time moves from noise toward data.

{% include figure.liquid path="assets/img/blogs/flowmap/diffusion.svg" mobile_path="assets/img/blogs/flowmap/diffusion-mobile.svg" alt="A Gaussian noise cloud becomes two data clusters as time decreases from one to zero." caption="Toy marginals of the linear interpolation. Color tracks the original data cluster." width=960 height=310 mobile_width=400 mobile_height=810 zoomable=true avoid_scaling=true %}

The network sees only $(x_t,t)$, so it learns $v(x,t)=\mathbb E[v_c\mid x_t=x]$. This is the **marginal velocity**, averaged over the endpoint pairs that could produce that state. As the sampler moves, the contributing pairs change, and the average direction can turn. Consequently, straight training segments can produce curved sampling trajectories—a key reason large sampling steps are difficult.

<h2 id="learning-the-jump">2. From local steps to a flow map</h2>

### Why a larger Euler step misses

Euler sampling takes the current velocity and holds it fixed across an interval: $x_s\approx x_t+(s-t)v(x_t,t)$. This works locally, where the direction changes little. Over a longer interval, the true trajectory can bend away from that initial tangent. A more accurate velocity network does not remove this integration error.

{% include figure.liquid path="assets/img/blogs/flowmap/transport.svg" mobile_path="assets/img/blogs/flowmap/transport-mobile.svg" alt="Orange Euler extrapolation misses the destination of a curved blue path. The teal flow-map arrow connects the endpoints." width=960 height=440 mobile_width=400 mobile_height=420 zoomable=true avoid_scaling=true %}

For a concrete example, take $\dot x=x$, starting at $x_1=1$. The exact destination is $x_0=e^{-1}\approx0.368$. One Euler step gives $1-1=0$, despite using the exact velocity. More steps reduce the error:

| Method                            | Predicted $x_0$ |
| --------------------------------- | --------------: |
| 1 Euler step                      |               0 |
| 2 Euler steps                     |           0.250 |
| 4 Euler steps                     |           0.316 |
| Exact map: $\Phi(x,t,s)=e^{s-t}x$ |           0.368 |

### The map predicts a destination

A **flow map** $\Phi(x_t,t,s)$ returns the state at destination time $s$, given the current state and time. It represents the result of integration:

$$
\Phi(x_t,t,s)=x_t+\int_t^s v(x_\tau,\tau)\,d\tau.
$$

The teal arrow above is the net displacement between endpoints; the underlying trajectory can still curve. An exact map leaves the state unchanged when $s=t$, and a direct jump agrees with two consecutive jumps through an intermediate time $r$:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\Phi(x,t,t)=x,\\
\Phi(x,t,s)=\Phi\big(\Phi(x,t,r),r,s\big).
\end{gathered}
$$

These properties connect different interval lengths and provide constraints for learning a map. In the scalar example, learning $e^{s-t}x$ would let the model return the endpoint directly.

### Two related ways to simplify sampling

[Rectified Flow](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.03003) makes the trajectories easier to integrate. Its **reflow** procedure runs the current sampler to pair each noise sample with a generated endpoint, then retrains on straight interpolations between those pairs. Repeating this can straighten transport and improve coarse Euler sampling.

[Consistency Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.01469) instead learn a shared clean endpoint for states on the same ODE trajectory. During distillation, neighboring teacher states should produce matching predictions; a boundary condition anchors clean inputs to themselves. A standard consistency model fixes the destination at the clean endpoint, while a two-time flow map lets the sampler request an intermediate destination too.

<h2 id="meanflow">3. MeanFlow: learning average velocity</h2>

### Replace the integral with an average

[MeanFlow](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.13447) parameterizes a jump using its **average velocity** $u$ over $[r,t]$. Multiplying that average by the interval length gives displacement:

$$
(t-r)u(x_t,r,t)=\int_r^t v(x_\tau,\tau)\,d\tau.
$$

{% include figure.liquid path="assets/img/blogs/flowmap/meanflow-jvp.svg" mobile_path="assets/img/blogs/flowmap/meanflow-jvp-mobile.svg" alt="The area under a changing velocity equals a rectangle with height equal to average velocity." width=960 height=345 mobile_width=400 mobile_height=660 zoomable=true avoid_scaling=true %}

The rectangle and curved area match even though their heights differ point by point. Once the network predicts $u$, sampling is simply $x_r=x_t-(t-r)u_\theta(x_t,r,t)$.

### Learn the average from local supervision

Computing the integral for every training target would require trajectories. MeanFlow avoids this by differentiating the identity with $r$ fixed:

$$
u+(t-r)\frac{du}{dt}=v,
\qquad \frac{du}{dt}=J_xu\,v+\partial_tu.
$$

The derivative changes both the starting state and its time. A **Jacobian-vector product (JVP)** evaluates this combined change without forming the full Jacobian $J_xu$. For inputs $(x,r,t)$, its direction is $(v,0,1)$.

### Training and one-step sampling

Training substitutes the sampled conditional velocity $v_c$ and constructs a corrected regression target:

$$
\begin{gathered}
D_\theta=\operatorname{JVP}[u_\theta;(v_c,0,1)],\\
\mathcal L=\mathbb E\left\|u_\theta-\operatorname{sg}\left[v_c-(t-r)D_\theta\right]\right\|^2.
\end{gathered}
$$

Here $\operatorname{sg}$ stops gradients through the target, avoiding backpropagation through the JVP. At $r=t$, the correction vanishes and training becomes ordinary velocity regression. Longer intervals teach the average needed for larger jumps. One-step generation sets $(t,r)=(1,0)$; inference needs one prediction of $u_\theta$, without a JVP. MeanFlow can train from scratch without a teacher.

<h2 id="training-refinements">4. Making flow-map training easier</h2>

### iMF: improve the derivative direction

The JVP multiplies a velocity by the network's Jacobian. If that velocity has high variance, the Jacobian can amplify it. [Improved MeanFlow (iMF)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.02012) replaces the sampled endpoint difference in the JVP with a predicted marginal velocity. It fits local velocity through a compound prediction consisting of the average velocity plus its derivative correction. This keeps the finite-interval identity while reducing noise in the derivative direction.

The method also conditions on guidance strength. **Classifier-free guidance** normally combines conditional and unconditional predictions, adding inference work. Learning a scale-conditioned guided field allows the student to vary guidance strength while retaining a single prediction per step.

### Curricula: establish local motion first

[AlphaFlow](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.20771) analyzes a conflict between fitting trajectory velocity and enforcing trajectory consistency. Its objective family connects these constraints, and a curriculum shifts emphasis from velocity fitting toward MeanFlow. [Accelerated MeanFlow training](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.19065) reaches a related practical conclusion: establish instantaneous velocity and short-interval averages before emphasizing long intervals. These methods change the order and weighting of training tasks; they help the network learn large jumps from an already useful local field.

### Decoupled MeanFlow: reuse a pretrained representation

A pretrained flow transformer already extracts useful features from a noisy state. [Decoupled MeanFlow](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.24474) reuses that computation by conditioning early blocks on current time and final blocks on destination time. The early representation describes the input; the later blocks turn it into the requested jump. Flow Matching pretraining can therefore precede the more expensive flow-map training, concentrating derivative-based fine-tuning on a model that already understands the data.

### TVM: differentiate the other endpoint

MeanFlow changes the start while holding the destination time fixed. [Terminal Velocity Matching (TVM)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.19797) holds the input fixed and changes the destination. For displacement $f(x_t,t,s)=\Phi(x_t,t,s)-x_t$, the destination-time derivative must equal the velocity at the endpoint:

$$
\partial_s f(x_t,t,s)=v\big(x_t+f(x_t,t,s),s\big).
$$

{% include figure.liquid path="assets/img/blogs/flowmap/endpoint-derivatives.svg" mobile_path="assets/img/blogs/flowmap/endpoint-derivatives-mobile.svg" alt="MeanFlow changes the starting point along the path while the destination stays fixed. TVM fixes the start and changes the destination." width=960 height=365 mobile_width=400 mobile_height=670 zoomable=true avoid_scaling=true %}

TVM parameterizes $f_\theta=(s-t)F_\theta$, enforcing zero displacement when the two times match. It trains the terminal derivative against a stopped target from an exponential moving average of the velocity model, with Flow Matching supervision anchoring local velocity. Unlike MeanFlow's detached JVP target, TVM backpropagates through its derivative prediction. That changes the training memory and autodiff requirements; sampling still only evaluates the learned jump.

<h2 id="distribution-matching">5. Matching the generated distribution</h2>

### DMD: compare where samples accumulate

Learning transport constrains individual jumps. A fast generator can still place too many samples in some regions and too few in others. [Distribution Matching Distillation (DMD)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.18828) supervises this population-level error. A teacher denoiser estimates the target score $s_{p,t}$, while an auxiliary denoiser learns the current generator score $s_{q,t}$. Both evaluate the same noisy generated sample, so their difference compares the two distributions at the same location and noise level.

{% include figure.liquid path="assets/img/blogs/flowmap/dmd.svg" mobile_path="assets/img/blogs/flowmap/dmd-mobile.svg" alt="The target Gaussian is centered at minus one and the student at plus one. Score-difference descent moves samples left toward the target." caption="Toy Gaussians: p = N(−1, 1), q = N(+1, 1), at one noise level." width=960 height=360 mobile_width=400 mobile_height=395 zoomable=true avoid_scaling=true %}

The toy student sits to the right of the target. Here $s_{p,t}-s_{q,t}=-2$, so gradient descent moves samples left toward the target. For generated samples $G_\theta(z)$ noised as $x_t=\alpha_tG_\theta(z)+\sigma_t\epsilon$, the distribution-loss gradient is

$$
\nabla_\theta\mathcal L=
\mathbb E\left[w(t)\alpha_t J_\theta G_\theta(z)^\top
(s_{q,t}-s_{p,t})\right].
$$

Here $w(t)$ weights noise levels, and the generator Jacobian translates the score difference into a parameter update. Training alternates fitting the auxiliary denoiser and updating the generator with that difference detached. Original DMD also adds paired regression to stabilize the student. The auxiliary denoiser must keep tracking the generator as its distribution changes.

### VSD and SiD: related signals, different objectives

[Variational Score Distillation (VSD)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16213) applies distribution supervision to text-to-3D. A differentiable renderer produces images from scene parameters and camera views; an auxiliary denoiser learns the distribution of those rendered images. Comparing it with a pretrained teacher gives a direction that propagates through the renderer into the scene. Accounting for the current rendered distribution replaces the sampled-noise reference used by standard Score Distillation Sampling (SDS).

[Score identity Distillation (SiD)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.04057) starts from a squared score-mismatch objective. It uses denoising identities to express that mismatch through residuals involving generated samples and teacher predictions. SiD also alternates an auxiliary denoiser with a generator, but its generator loss differentiates through score-network inputs. The similar two-denoiser setup therefore has different gradient paths from DMD, affecting implementation and training cost.

### Adversarial supervision: learn a real/fake signal

A discriminator can learn distribution feedback directly from real and generated examples. [Adversarial Diffusion Distillation (ADD)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17042) combines image-feature discrimination with diffusion-teacher supervision: one encourages realistic appearance, while the other constrains the student's predictions. [Latent Adversarial Diffusion Distillation (LADD)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.12015) extracts discriminator features from a pretrained latent diffusion model. Keeping this supervision in latent space avoids repeatedly decoding images and running a separate image feature extractor.

[DiffRatio](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08005) connects classification back to scores. For a classifier $D_t(x)=P(\mathrm{real}\mid x,t)$ trained on balanced real/fake batches, the optimal log odds recover the density ratio:

$$
\log\frac{q_t(x)}{p_t(x)}=
\log\frac{1-D_t(x)}{D_t(x)}.
$$

Taking its spatial gradient gives $s_{q,t}-s_{p,t}$. A time-conditioned classifier can therefore estimate the score difference directly, supplying generator supervision without a pretrained teacher score. Accuracy now depends on the classifier and its input gradients.

<h2 id="extensions">6. Different training states, posterior samples, and video</h2>

Once a fast sampler works, its surrounding computation matters: obtaining training examples, evaluating possible completions, or repeatedly running a video backbone. The methods below change the training inputs or computation around the jump; long video also requires learning from generated history.

### FreeFlow: query the teacher on student states

Distillation data can differ from a teacher's output distribution, particularly after guidance or fine-tuning. [FreeFlow](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.19428) avoids that mismatch by sampling from the noise prior and querying the teacher along the student's predicted path. One constraint teaches the teacher's generation trajectory; another corrects errors using the noising behavior of generated samples. The training states therefore follow what the student actually produces. Data-free here means no external dataset during distillation—the teacher and auxiliary estimation still require compute.

### Meta Flow Maps: sample alternative completions

A deterministic map returns one endpoint for a fixed state. Reward-based steering may need several plausible completions to estimate how promising that state is. [Meta Flow Maps](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.14430) learn a conditional sampler for the clean posterior $p(x_0\mid x_t)$. Fresh auxiliary noise $\eta_k$ selects different completions while the observed state and time remain fixed:

$$
x_0^{(k)}=H_\theta(\eta_k;x_t,t),
\qquad \eta_k\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal N(0,I).
$$

{% include figure.liquid path="assets/img/blogs/flowmap/posterior.svg" mobile_path="assets/img/blogs/flowmap/posterior-mobile.svg" alt="A deterministic map has one endpoint per state. A Meta Flow Map uses fresh noise to sample multiple clean completions from the same state." width=960 height=300 mobile_width=400 mobile_height=520 zoomable=true avoid_scaling=true %}

This posterior comes from the noising/interpolation process: several clean examples can explain the same intermediate observation. The model learns an auxiliary flow conditioned on $(x_t,t)$. Its samples provide differentiable estimates of conditional rewards, reducing the need to run many full trajectories inside each steering step. This changes the object being learned: the output represents a distribution of compatible clean samples.

### TMD: spend video compute on a smaller transition head

Video transformers make every full-model evaluation expensive. [Transition Matching Distillation (TMD)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.09881) splits the model into a large feature-extracting backbone and a small conditional flow head. At an outer sampling step, the backbone supplies features that the head can reuse for inner transition updates. After learning transitions, distribution matching refines the generated outputs. The split gives a way to improve a transition without repeating the entire backbone for every inner update.

### AAPT: train with the history inference will produce

Autoregressive video adds another mismatch: training may supply reference history, while inference must continue from generated history. Small errors can then become the context for later mistakes. [Autoregressive Adversarial Post-Training (AAPT)](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/3a9468a918fc65dc9ce7b7bd99f4f0ef-Paper-Conference.pdf) adapts and distills a video model for causal generation, then adversarially trains its rollouts. **Student forcing** feeds generated chunks into subsequent chunks, exposing training to the histories the model creates.

Causal attention and a key/value cache reuse earlier context. The discriminator supplies feedback on generated video segments, while rollout training addresses accumulated error over time. Evaluation consequently needs long sequences: sharp individual frames do not establish consistent objects, motion, or scenes.

Across these methods, report **network function evaluations (NFE) and full generation latency** together. Guidance branches, transition heads, and latent decoding all consume time. Hold prompts, resolution, and guidance fixed, then compare fidelity and diversity alongside speed; for video, also measure consistency over the full rollout.
