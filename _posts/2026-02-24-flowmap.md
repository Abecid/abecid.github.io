A velocity field tells a sampler where to move next. A **flow map** predicts where it should land after an entire time interval. That distinction is the route from dozens of denoising calls to one or a few—but learning the jump is harder than increasing the solver’s step size and hoping for the best.

There are two problems to solve: **transport error** (did the jump follow the intended dynamics?) and **distribution error** (do the resulting samples have the right statistics?). Derivative and consistency losses address transport; score and adversarial losses provide distribution supervision. The useful comparison is which error a method supervises, which derivative it needs, and what remains expensive at inference.

**Conventions used here:** $x_0$ is data, $x_1$ is noise, and generation runs from $t=1$ toward $t=0$. We write $v(x_t,t)=dx_t/dt$ in the increasing-time direction, so a sampling step has a negative time increment. Some papers reverse these conventions; the equations below are translated where needed. $\operatorname{sg}$ means stop-gradient: preserve a value in the forward pass, treat it as constant in the backward pass. **NFE** is the number of network function evaluations during sampling.

## 1. Foundations

### 1.1 Diffusion

Diffusion training creates noisy examples from clean data and teaches a network to undo the corruption. For Gaussian noising,

$$
x_t=\alpha_t x_0+\sigma_t\epsilon,
\qquad \epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I).
$$

The schedule controls the signal and noise amplitudes. In discrete DDPM, $\alpha_t=\sqrt{\bar a_t}$ and $\sigma_t=\sqrt{1-\bar a_t}$, with $\bar a_t=\prod_{j=1}^t(1-\beta_j)$ and per-step noise variance $\beta_j$. This closed form lets training sample any noise level directly. It does not have to simulate every preceding noising step. [DDPM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11239).

A common objective is $\mathbb E\|\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)-\epsilon\|^2$. Its optimum predicts the conditional mean noise. For $\sigma_t>0$, this determines the **score**, the direction of increasing log density:

$$
s_t(x)=\nabla_x\log p_t(x)
=-\frac{\mathbb E[\epsilon\mid x_t=x]}{\sigma_t}.
$$

A score is a vector field, not a scalar rating of how nice an image looks. For a forward stochastic differential equation $dx=f(x,t)dt+g(t)dW_t$, with drift $f$, noise amplitude $g$, and Brownian motion $W_t$, the corresponding probability-flow ODE has drift $f-\tfrac12g^2s_t$. Under the usual regularity assumptions, the ODE and SDE have the same time marginals, but different individual paths. This gives a deterministic sampling route—and an integration problem to accelerate. [Probability-flow ODE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13456).

### 1.2 Flow Matching

[Flow Matching](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.02747) trains the velocity field directly. For independent data and noise endpoints, choose

$$
x_t=(1-t)x_0+t x_1,
\qquad v_c=x_1-x_0,
\qquad
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{FM}}=\mathbb E\|v_\theta(x_t,t)-v_c\|^2.
$$

Here $v_c$ is the velocity of the **sampled conditional path**. The network only sees $(x_t,t)$, not the endpoints that produced it. Squared-error regression therefore learns the **marginal velocity**

$$
v(x,t)=\mathbb E[v_c\mid x_t=x].
$$

That conditional-versus-marginal distinction matters. Individual training segments are straight, but their averaged field can produce curved ODE trajectories. A straight interpolation formula is not a guarantee of straight sampling paths.

At inference, Euler sampling uses $x_s\approx x_t+(s-t)v_\theta(x_t,t)$ for $s<t$. This approximation freezes the tangent across the interval. If the velocity changes substantially along the path, a large step misses the endpoint even when the local velocity prediction is perfect.

### 1.3 Rectified Flow

[Rectified Flow](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.03003) makes coarse integration easier by changing the endpoint coupling. In **reflow**, first run the current ODE to pair each noise sample with its generated endpoint. Then train on straight interpolations between those coupled endpoints. Repeating this procedure can straighten the learned transport and reduce discretization error.

The mechanism is geometric: improve the paths that the solver must integrate. A flow-map model instead learns the finite-time result of integration. These approaches can complement each other; neither removes errors in the underlying learned distribution.

### 1.4 Flow Map

For an ODE $\dot x_t=v(x_t,t)$, define its flow map as

$$
\Phi(x_t,t,s)=x_t+\int_t^s v(x_\tau,\tau)\,d\tau.
$$

It has two useful constraints:

$$
\Phi(x,t,t)=x,
\qquad
\Phi(x,t,s)=\Phi\big(\Phi(x,t,r),r,s\big).
$$

The first is the identity boundary. The second says a direct jump agrees with two jumps through an intermediate time. A learned map approximates this solution operator; it does not automatically satisfy composition because its inputs happen to include two times.

**A worked example.** Take the scalar ODE $\dot x=x$, starting at $x_1=1$ and integrating backward to zero. The exact solution is $x_t=e^{t-1}$, so $x_0=e^{-1}\approx0.368$. One Euler step gives $1-1=0$. The velocity was correct; the extrapolation was not. Two equal Euler steps give $(1-1/2)^2=0.25$; four give $(1-1/4)^4\approx0.316$.

{% include figure.liquid path="assets/img/blogs/flowmap/integration-example.svg" mobile_path="assets/img/blogs/flowmap/integration-example-mobile.svg" alt="Exact exponential trajectory compared with one, two, and four explicit Euler steps backward in time; endpoint error decreases with step count." caption="Figure 3. This is an analytic ODE example, not a model benchmark." width=1500 height=615 mobile_width=780 mobile_height=960 zoomable=true avoid_scaling=true %}

For this toy, the exact map is $\Phi(x,t,s)=e^{s-t}x$. A model that learns this map can jump directly. A model that learns only $v(x,t)=x$ still needs a suitable integrator. The advantage comes from learning the finite interval, not from taking a larger Euler step.

### 1.5 Consistency Models

A [consistency model](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.01469) maps states on the same ODE trajectory to a common clean endpoint:

$$
C_\theta(x_t,t)\approx C_{\bar\theta}(x_s,s),
\qquad C_\theta(x_0,0)=x_0.
$$

In distillation, a teacher solver supplies neighboring trajectory states, and $\bar\theta$ denotes a stabilized target network. The boundary is essential: agreement alone permits a constant output. Original implementations anchor at a small positive minimum noise rather than a singular zero-noise endpoint.

A standard endpoint consistency model predicts one destination. A two-time flow map predicts a requested destination time. Both move beyond local velocity supervision, but they expose different sampling controls.

## 2. MeanFlow Family

### 2.1 MeanFlow

[MeanFlow](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.13447) parameterizes the **average velocity** over an interval. Along an ODE trajectory,

$$
(t-r)u(x_t,r,t)=\int_r^t v(x_\tau,\tau)\,d\tau,
\qquad x_r=x_t-(t-r)u(x_t,r,t).
$$

This is a time average along a moving trajectory—not the average of the velocities at its two endpoints. Differentiating the first equation with respect to $t$, while holding $r$ fixed, gives

$$
u+(t-r)\frac{du}{dt}=v,
\qquad
\frac{du}{dt}=J_xu\,v+\partial_tu.
$$

{% include figure.liquid path="assets/img/blogs/flowmap/meanflow-jvp.svg" mobile_path="assets/img/blogs/flowmap/meanflow-jvp-mobile.svg" alt="The area under a changing velocity curve equals a rectangle of height u over the same interval. Extending the interval adds a thin strip at the endpoint velocity." caption="Figure 4. Blue area = dashed rectangle area = displacement. Extending t adds the orange strip at the local velocity. The rectangle changes in both width and height u: the product rule behind MeanFlow. Shown for one scalar velocity component." width=1260 height=585 mobile_width=780 mobile_height=585 zoomable=true avoid_scaling=true %}

The **Jacobian-vector product (JVP)** computes $J_xu\,v+\partial_tu$ without constructing the full Jacobian. Its tangent is $(v,0,1)$ when the inputs are ordered $(x,r,t)$. Differentiating only the time embedding drops the spatial term. The state moves when time moves; the JVP must include both changes.

The practical MeanFlow training residual uses the sampled conditional velocity $v_c$:

$$
D_\theta=\operatorname{JVP}\big[u_\theta;(v_c,0,1)\big],
\qquad
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{MF}}
=\mathbb E\left\|u_\theta-
\operatorname{sg}\left[v_c-(t-r)D_\theta\right]\right\|^2.
$$

On the diagonal $r=t$, the interval term vanishes and supervision reduces to ordinary velocity regression. Away from it, the derivative constrains finite-time transport. The network must learn both regimes; good diagonal predictions alone do not establish a good one-step sampler.

Once trained, sampling is $x_r=x_t-(t-r)u_\theta(x_t,r,t)$. One-step generation sets $(t,r)=(1,0)$. The JVP is a **training** cost; inference needs the average-velocity prediction only. MeanFlow can train from scratch and does not inherently require a teacher.

### 2.2 iMF: reduce noise in the JVP tangent

[iMF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.02012) moves the derivative correction into a compound velocity prediction:

$$
V_\theta=u_\theta+(t-r)\operatorname{sg}
\left[\operatorname{JVP}[u_\theta;(v_\theta,0,1)]\right],
\qquad
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{iMF}}=\mathbb E\|V_\theta-v_c\|^2.
$$

The important change is the tangent: a predicted marginal velocity $v_\theta$, rather than the noisy endpoint difference $v_c$. Merely moving the same detached term across the equals sign would preserve the original gradient. Changing the tangent avoids feeding conditional-target variance through the Jacobian.

The paper also conditions on the guidance scale, so one trained model can support different inference guidance strengths. **Classifier-free guidance (CFG)** combines conditional and unconditional fields, conventionally $v_w=v_{\mathrm{uncond}}+w(v_{\mathrm{cond}}-v_{\mathrm{uncond}})$. Distilling that field into a scale-conditioned student can avoid two separate inference passes. In-context conditioning represents times, class information, and guidance variables as transformer tokens.

### 2.3 AlphaFlow: learn local motion before long jumps

[AlphaFlow](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.20771) separates the MeanFlow training gradient into trajectory velocity fitting and trajectory consistency. Its analysis finds conflict between these components, helping explain why small-gap or diagonal supervision can stabilize training.

The method introduces a finite-interval objective family controlled by $\alpha$: trajectory flow matching at one end, a Shortcut-style objective at an intermediate setting, and the MeanFlow gradient in a limiting case. A curriculum moves from velocity fitting toward the consistency-dominated regime. This is a gradient-level relationship; taking the limit in a displayed $1/\alpha$ loss without treating constants and stopped gradients carefully is misleading.

The practical knob is the order in which constraints become difficult. Establish local velocity before asking the same network to solve long intervals reliably.

### 2.4 Spend training effort on the right intervals

[Understanding, Accelerating, and Improving MeanFlow Training](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.19065) attacks two training bottlenecks: slow velocity learning and difficult large-gap average-velocity learning. It combines diffusion-style time sampling or loss weighting with a curriculum over temporal gaps.

For the progressive weighting described in the paper,

$$
\beta(\Delta t,a)=1-a+\lambda a(1-\Delta t),
\qquad a=1-i/T.
$$

Here $i$ is the optimizer step, $T$ is the total training budget, and $\lambda$ normalizes the initial weighting. At initialization $a=1$, short intervals receive more weight; at the end $a=0$, weighting is uniform. Calling $a$ “training progress” without specifying its direction would invert the recipe.

Loss weighting and time sampling both change where training effort goes, but they interact differently with adaptive normalization. Their effects should be checked separately before combining them.

### 2.5 Decoupled MeanFlow: reuse the representation

[Decoupled MeanFlow](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.24474) changes **where** time conditioning enters a pretrained flow transformer. Early blocks encode the current state at time $t$; later blocks decode toward destination time $r$:

$$
u_\theta(x_t,t,r)=g_\theta\big(f_\theta(x_t,t),r\big).
$$

The paper demonstrates direct conversion through this conditioning split and improves low-NFE generation with flow-map fine-tuning. The efficient training route is FM pretraining followed by conversion and MeanFlow training. This spends expensive JVP training on interval prediction after a useful representation already exists. Direct conversion results do not imply that an arbitrary pretrained model becomes a strong one-step generator without adaptation.

| Variant                       | Main intervention                                       | What to inspect during training                  |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| MeanFlow                      | Average-velocity identity with a stopped target         | Diagonal error versus long-interval error        |
| iMF                           | Predicted marginal tangent; scale conditioning          | JVP variance and behavior across guidance scales |
| AlphaFlow                     | Curriculum between velocity and consistency constraints | Conflict between the two gradient components     |
| Accelerated MeanFlow training | Time/gap sampling and weighting                         | Where the training budget is spent               |
| Decoupled MeanFlow            | Current-time encoder, destination-time decoder          | Representation quality and fine-tuning cost      |

## 3. Score Distillation

Transport supervision says how states should move. Distribution supervision asks whether the generated population matches the target. This distinction matters when a fast sampler produces coherent but oversmoothed samples, loses modes, or accumulates errors away from the teacher’s training states.

Write $q_\theta$ for the generator distribution and $p$ for the desired distribution. Add Gaussian noise to $x=G_\theta(z)$ to obtain $x_t=\alpha_t x+\sigma_t\epsilon$. For a fixed target distribution, a weighted KL objective has the reparameterized gradient

$$
\nabla_\theta\mathcal L
=\mathbb E\left[
 w(t)\alpha_t J_\theta G_\theta(z)^\top
 \big(s_{q,t}(x_t)-s_{p,t}(x_t)\big)
\right].
$$

Here $s_{q,t}$ and $s_{p,t}$ are the two noisy-distribution scores. This is the **gradient**; gradient descent moves in its negative direction. Noise makes the distributions smoother and gives comparison signals at several scales. A single noise level or an inaccurate score estimator can still leave substantial distribution error.

### 3.1 Variational Score Distillation (VSD)

In text-to-3D, replace $G_\theta$ with a differentiable renderer $g(\theta,c)$, where $\theta$ contains scene parameters and $c$ is a camera. SDS uses a pretrained noise predictor and the sampled noise to form the update direction. [VSD / ProlificDreamer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16213) instead models a distribution over scene parameters and learns the score of the resulting rendered images.

The operational difference is

$$
\text{SDS signal: }\epsilon_{\mathrm{teacher}}-\epsilon,
\qquad
\text{VSD signal: }\epsilon_{\mathrm{teacher}}-\epsilon_{\mathrm{rendered}}.
$$

VSD alternates fitting the rendered-image denoiser, often a low-rank adaptation of the teacher, with updating scene particles through the renderer. The auxiliary model accounts for the current rendered distribution. “One particle” here means one scene parameter vector, not one scalar or one rendered view.

### 3.2 DMD (Distribution Matching Distillation)

[DMD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.18828) applies the score-difference gradient to a fast image generator. The teacher estimates the target score; an auxiliary denoiser estimates the current generator score. Both evaluate the **same noisy generated sample**.

{% include figure.liquid path="assets/img/blogs/flowmap/dmd.svg" mobile_path="assets/img/blogs/flowmap/dmd-mobile.svg" alt="A student Gaussian lies to the right of the target Gaussian. Negative score-difference arrows move samples left; the dashed distribution shows a partial shift toward the target." caption="Figure 5. Why the sign matters. At one noise level, take unit-variance Gaussians centered at −1 (target) and +1 (student). Their score difference is +2 everywhere, so descent moves left. The dashed curve shows a unit shift in sample space. DMD projects this signal through the generator Jacobian; a neural-network update need not translate every sample equally." width=1260 height=600 mobile_width=825 mobile_height=630 zoomable=true avoid_scaling=true %}

The loop is:

1. Generate samples and detach them to train the auxiliary denoiser with ordinary denoising regression.
2. Generate a fresh batch; evaluate both denoisers at matched noise levels.
3. Build the score-difference direction and backpropagate it through the generator. For this update, DMD treats that direction as fixed rather than differentiating through the score networks.

Original DMD also uses paired regression to preserve coarse structure and stabilize training. The distribution gradient does not require differentiating a long teacher rollout. Its weakness is estimator tracking: a stale auxiliary model reports yesterday’s generator distribution.

### 3.3 SiD (Score identity Distillation)

[SiD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.04057) targets a squared score difference on noised generated samples—a Fisher-divergence-type objective—rather than simply taking the KL gradient above. Naively squaring two imperfect score estimates can create a poor generator objective.

The useful identity is easiest to see with additive noising $x_t=x+\sigma_t\epsilon$. Let $D_p,D_q$ denote the ideal teacher and generator denoising means. Then

$$
s_{p,t}-s_{q,t}=\frac{D_p(x_t)-D_q(x_t)}{\sigma_t^2},
\qquad D_q(x_t)=\mathbb E[x\mid x_t].
$$

Conditioning on $x_t$ also gives

$$
\mathbb E\!\left[(D_p-D_q)^\top(D_p-x)\right]
=\mathbb E\|D_p-D_q\|^2.
$$

This replaces a squared difference with a projected denoising residual. SiD uses score identities to construct practical projected/fused objectives while alternating auxiliary-denoiser and generator updates. Unlike DMD’s detached direction, these generator losses involve input derivatives through score networks. The distinction affects autodiff support and memory, even when both methods have a teacher and a fake-data denoiser in their diagrams.

## 4. Adversarial Distillation

A discriminator learns a real-versus-generated signal instead of supplying a pretrained score. The original GAN intuition still applies: discriminator quality, regularization, and update balance determine whether its gradient helps. A sharper sample is not evidence that mode coverage improved.

### 4.1 Adversarial Diffusion Distillation (ADD)

[ADD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17042) combines a few-step student with two signals: adversarial supervision on image features and teacher-based diffusion supervision. The discriminator supplies perceptual detail; the diffusion teacher constrains the student’s predictions. These signals need not prefer identical outputs, so their relative weighting is part of the method.

Conceptually, the generator minimizes $\mathcal L_G=\lambda_{\mathrm{adv}}\mathcal L_{\mathrm{adv}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{distill}}\mathcal L_{\mathrm{distill}}$. This is an objective decomposition, not an implementation-ready loss: the feature extractor, teacher target, noise distribution, and discriminator regularizer still matter.

### 4.2 LADD (Latent Adversarial Diffusion Distillation)

[LADD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.12015) moves discrimination into features from a pretrained **latent diffusion model**. This avoids repeatedly decoding latents to pixels and then running a separate image feature extractor for the adversarial signal.

Generate a latent, perturb real and generated latents at sampled noise levels, extract diffusion features, and alternate discriminator and student updates. The feature backbone remains fixed. Noise level changes the granularity of supervision: low noise exposes local detail, while higher noise emphasizes larger-scale structure.

The original method is high-resolution image distillation. Autoregressive video rollout is a separate extension, discussed below.

### 4.3 DiffRatio

[DiffRatio](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08005) estimates the score difference **directly**, using a classifier trained on noised real and generated data. It does not require a pretrained teacher score for the generator’s supervision.

For a balanced classifier with $D_t(x)=P(\mathrm{real}\mid x,t)$, the optimal discriminator gives

$$
\log\frac{q_t(x)}{p_t(x)}
=\log\frac{1-D_t(x)}{D_t(x)},
\qquad
s_{q,t}-s_{p,t}
=\nabla_x\log\frac{q_t}{p_t}.
$$

Alternate classifier fitting with generator updates using the spatial gradient of this log ratio. Class imbalance requires the corresponding prior correction. The method avoids subtracting two independently learned score fields, but its classifier and input gradients still have estimation error. Its supervision comes from classifying noisy populations, with no paired teacher trajectory required.

### 4.4 APT and the autoregressive AAPT extension

**Adversarial post-training (APT)** applies distribution supervision after a diffusion model has been converted to a fast generator. The practical rationale is that low-NFE regression can preserve coarse behavior while washing out details; a discriminator supplies an additional signal. It does not follow that adversarial training is necessary for every successful distilled model.

For streaming video, [AAPT](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/3a9468a918fc65dc9ce7b7bd99f4f0ef-Paper-Conference.pdf) adapts a pretrained video diffusion model to causal chunk generation, distills the sampling process, then adversarially trains generated rollouts. Causal attention and a **key/value cache** retain earlier context without recomputing it at every chunk.

The crucial distinction is **teacher forcing versus student forcing**. Teacher forcing supplies reference history when predicting the next chunk. Student forcing feeds back the model’s generated history, exposing training to the mistakes it will encounter at inference. Discriminating shorter segments of a longer rollout makes supervision practical, but convincing short windows do not prove long-range consistency. Track objects, camera motion, and drift over the whole sequence. The furniture should not respawn between shots unless that was the prompt.

### 4.5 Comparisons

| Signal     | Learned estimator                              | Generator receives                                      |
| ---------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| DMD / VSD  | Score of current generated or rendered samples | Difference from a pretrained target score               |
| SiD        | Generated-data denoiser                        | A score-identity loss with input derivatives            |
| ADD / LADD | Discriminator on selected features             | Adversarial gradient; ADD also uses teacher supervision |
| DiffRatio  | Time-conditioned real/fake classifier          | Gradient of a learned log density ratio                 |
| AAPT       | Discriminator trained on video rollouts        | Feedback on samples conditioned on generated history    |

## 5. Changing the supervision

The remaining methods change a specific part of the problem: the supervision states, the stochastic object being learned, the differentiated endpoint, or the location of expensive derivatives inside a video model.

### 5.1 FreeFlow: flow-map distillation without data

[FreeFlow](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.19428) addresses **teacher–data mismatch**: an available dataset may differ from a teacher’s actual output distribution, especially after guidance or fine-tuning. It samples from the prior and queries the teacher along the student’s own predicted path.

Using this post’s increasing-time velocity $v$, define generation progress $\delta=1-t$ and

$$
f_\theta(z,\delta)=z+\delta F_\theta(z,\delta),
\qquad z\sim p_1.
$$

The ideal generating-velocity constraint is

$$
F_\theta+\delta\partial_\delta F_\theta
=-v\big(f_\theta(z,\delta),1-\delta\big).
$$

The minus sign comes from $dt/d\delta=-1$. The paper calls the generation-directed teacher field $u=-v$. Its prediction objective uses a detached target built from that teacher field and the interval derivative.

Finite-capacity predictions can drift. A second correction signal compares the noising velocity induced by generated samples with the teacher’s noising behavior, linking path prediction with marginal-distribution correction. Data-free here means no external dataset during distillation; the teacher and an auxiliary estimate of student-induced noising behavior still cost compute.

### 5.2 Meta Flow Maps

[Meta Flow Maps](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.14430) target a different object: **posterior sampling** from an intermediate state. A deterministic endpoint map returns one completion. Reward alignment may need many plausible completions to estimate how promising the current state is.

Write the clean endpoint as $x_{\mathrm{clean}}$ to avoid the paper’s opposite time convention. An MFM uses independent auxiliary noise $\eta$ to produce

$$
x_{\mathrm{clean}}^{(k)}=H_\theta(\eta_k;x_t,t),
\qquad \eta_k\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal N(0,I).
$$

The target law is $p(x_{\mathrm{clean}}\mid x_t)$ under the noising/interpolation joint. This is different from the single endpoint determined by a fixed state on a deterministic ODE trajectory. For fixed context $(x_t,t)$, the method learns an **auxiliary conditional flow map** from fresh noise to that posterior. Diagonal supervision fits the auxiliary instantaneous velocity; consistency supervision teaches finite intervals in the auxiliary clock. The auxiliary clock describes posterior sampling; the outer time specifies the observation being conditioned on.

For a reward $R$, these draws estimate the conditional expectation $K^{-1}\sum_k R(x_{\mathrm{clean}}^{(k)})$. The sampler provides a differentiable reparameterization, reducing the need for nested trajectory rollouts. Reward tilting may require a transformed reward, such as an exponential, depending on the alignment objective.

### 5.3 Terminal Velocity Matching (TVM)

[TVM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.19797) constrains the destination-time derivative of a displacement map. Set

$$
f_\theta(x_t,t,s)=(s-t)F_\theta(x_t,t,s),
\qquad
v_\theta(x_t,t)=F_\theta(x_t,t,t).
$$

The factor $(s-t)$ enforces zero displacement on the diagonal. For the exact map,

$$
\partial_s f(x_t,t,s)
=v\big(x_t+f(x_t,t,s),s\big).
$$

Unlike MeanFlow’s derivative along a moving starting state, $\partial_s$ holds $(x_t,t)$ fixed. The learned residual compares

$$
\underbrace{F_\theta+(s-t)\partial_sF_\theta}_{\text{terminal derivative}}
\quad\text{with}\quad
\underbrace{\operatorname{sg}\left[
 v_{\bar\theta}\big(x_t+\operatorname{sg}[f_\theta],s\big)
\right]}_{\text{stabilized endpoint velocity}}.
$$

An FM anchor trains the diagonal velocity from data. The proxy target uses an exponential moving average (EMA), while training differentiates through the derivative branch on the left. This requires backward differentiation through a JVP, a materially different kernel requirement from merely computing a detached JVP target.

For Lipschitz velocity models and the paper’s other assumptions, TVM relates integrated residual error to a Wasserstein-2 distribution bound. This motivates its normalization choices and derivative-compatible attention implementation. The bound depends on integrated error under its assumptions, so a small minibatch residual alone cannot establish sample quality.

Sampling simply adds $f_\theta$ over the chosen intervals. No training-time JVP is needed. With this post’s notation, TVM’s $F_\theta(x,t,s)$ and MeanFlow’s $u_\theta(x,s,t)$ both represent interval-average velocity; their **training constraints and gradient paths** differ.

### 5.4 Transition Matching Distillation

[TMD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.09881) adapts flow-map learning to large video transformers. Its transition-matching stage introduces a conditional inner flow map, with a transition head conditioned on features from the main backbone. This concentrates the flow-map learning problem in the transition component rather than treating every outer-model operation as an undifferentiated JVP problem.

The head’s parameterization preserves a useful relationship to the pretrained prediction head. Finite differences approximate derivative terms where exact JVP support is awkward with attention kernels and distributed execution. A finite difference substitutes perturbed forward evaluations for a derivative primitive; its step size trades truncation error against cancellation and numerical noise.

After transition pretraining, a DMD2-style stage combines distribution matching with adversarial refinement. The stages have different jobs: learn useful transitions, then improve their output distribution. Report temporal quality, training compute, and full generation latency—not just the denoiser’s NFE.

## 6. What to implement and measure

Choose the bottleneck before choosing the loss:

| Observation                                     | Candidate experiment                                 | What would falsify the explanation?                     |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Accurate local velocity, poor large jumps       | Flow-map or average-velocity objective               | Endpoint error remains after local error is controlled  |
| Noisy or slow MeanFlow training                 | Marginal tangent, interval curriculum, or FM warm-up | No gain at matched training compute                     |
| Coherent trajectories, weak sample distribution | DMD, SiD, or adversarial refinement                  | Fidelity improves only by sacrificing diversity         |
| Good short clips, drifting long rollouts        | Train on generated history                           | Long-horizon consistency still degrades                 |
| Missing or mismatched distillation data         | FreeFlow with a fixed teacher                        | Teacher capability is still lost on held-out conditions |
| Need alternative completions from one state     | Posterior sampling with MFM                          | Samples fail posterior coverage or calibration checks   |

Keep the teacher checkpoint, prompts, resolution, seeds, and guidance policy fixed when comparing samplers. Report both NFE and wall-clock time: two branches for guidance, an auxiliary head, the VAE, and output encoding all have costs that a step count can hide. Count the full pipeline when deciding whether a sampling method is faster.

Separate numerical checks from sample-quality evaluation. The former should exercise the identity boundary, time direction, JVP tangent, and gradient stops on a toy problem with an exact solution. The latter should include a held-out prompt set, distribution metrics, diversity, and video-specific temporal behavior where relevant. A few excellent samples explain what a model can do; they do not measure how often it does it.
